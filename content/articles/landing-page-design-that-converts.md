A landing page has one job. If you cannot state that job in a sentence, the page is not ready.

The best landing pages are not the most beautiful. They are the clearest. They answer the buyer's questions in the order the buyer asks them, then make the next step easy. Design makes that clarity feel credible.

Here is the framework we use.

## 1. One promise

The first screen should answer:

- What is this?
- Who is it for?
- What changes if I use it?
- What do I do next?

A useful promise is specific and believable. Compare:

- "Unlock your digital potential."
- "A five-page website for service businesses, launched in three weeks, with lead tracking."

The second is longer, but it does a job. It tells the right buyer they are in the right place and the wrong buyer to leave.

### A headline formula

You do not need a copywriting trick. Try:

**Outcome + audience + constraint**

- "A website that brings qualified leads — without a six-month build."
- "Custom operations dashboards for teams still living in spreadsheets."
- "Technical SEO for React sites that Google cannot read."

Then support it with a subhead that explains how, for whom, or what happens next.

## 2. Proof immediately after the claim

People do not believe claims. They believe evidence.

Proof can be:

- A case study with a before/after.
- A screenshot of the product or work.
- A client logo (with permission).
- A specific result.
- A testimonial from a real customer.
- A transparent process.

If you do not have client proof yet, show the work itself. An interface, a diagram, a sample deliverable, or a clear explanation of how you solve the problem is better than a vague badge.

Never invent reviews, logos, or numbers. One real proof point is worth more than a wall of decoration.

## 3. Handle objections in order

A landing page is a sequence of answers. The most common objections for service businesses:

- "Is this for someone like me?"
- "Will it work for my situation?"
- "How much does it cost?"
- "How long will it take?"
- "What do I have to do?"
- "What if it does not work?"
- "Why you instead of the alternative?"
- "What happens after I contact you?"

Design the page around those questions. Use specific sections, not generic feature grids.

A useful structure:

1. Hero: promise and primary CTA.
2. Proof strip: logos, stats, or evidence.
3. Problem: describe the cost of the status quo.
4. Solution: how the offer works.
5. What is included.
6. Process: what happens after contact.
7. Pricing signal or starting range.
8. Case study or example.
9. FAQ: the remaining objections.
10. Final CTA.

Not every page needs all ten. A high-ticket service usually needs more; a simple lead magnet needs less.

## 4. One primary action

Every page should have one action you want most. Repeat it, but do not compete with it.

- Primary: book a call, send a brief, start a WhatsApp conversation.
- Secondary: read a case study, see pricing, ask a question.

The secondary path is for people who are not ready. It should not look equally important.

On paid landing pages, remove the site navigation. On organic service pages, keep it. The goal and the traffic source should shape the layout.

## 5. Reduce friction

Friction is anything that makes the next step harder:

- A long form.
- Unclear fields.
- A calendar widget with no available times.
- A required account.
- A page that shifts while someone is tapping.
- A slow load.
- A vague success state.

The minimum viable form for a service business is usually name, email, and a short message. Phone and service can be optional. If you need qualification, ask in the reply, not in the form.

Form design has been studied extensively. The Nielsen Norman Group's [form design guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-form-design/) is a good starting point: visible labels, clear errors, and fewer fields.

## 6. Make the page fast

A landing page that takes four seconds to load loses people before the promise is read. Performance is part of conversion design.

- Optimize the hero image and do not lazy-load it.
- Use a modern image format.
- Load fonts with `display: swap`.
- Defer non-critical scripts.
- Reserve space for embeds and banners.
- Test on a mid-range phone.

The [Core Web Vitals guide](/blog/core-web-vitals-2026/) covers the technical details. For a campaign page, speed is not optional.

## 7. Write like a person

Avoid:

- "In today's fast-paced digital landscape."
- "We are passionate about delivering excellence."
- "Cutting-edge, seamless, innovative solutions."
- "Unlock," "elevate," "empower," "revolutionize."

Use:

- Specific nouns and verbs.
- Short sentences.
- Contractions.
- Concrete examples.
- The buyer's language, taken from sales calls.

Read the page out loud. If you would not say it to a client, rewrite it.

## 8. Use images with a job

Images should do one of four things:

- Show the product or work.
- Show the result.
- Show the process.
- Create the right mood.

A generic stock photo of people high-fiving does none of these. A real screenshot, a before/after, or a simple diagram does.

Use descriptive alt text for informative images. Decorative images can have empty alt. Optimize file size.

## 9. Design for scanning

Most visitors scan before they read.

- One idea per section.
- Descriptive headings.
- Short paragraphs.
- Bullet lists where they help.
- Visual hierarchy: one H1, clear H2s, one primary CTA.
- Generous spacing, but not so much that the page feels empty.
- Contrast that meets accessibility standards.

Design should guide the eye to the next action, not show off.

## 10. Test what matters

You can test:

- The headline.
- The offer.
- The primary CTA label.
- Form length.
- Social proof placement.
- Pricing presentation.
- The first screen layout.

But with low traffic, A/B tests can take months to reach significance. Start with qualitative fixes:

- Watch session recordings.
- Ask five buyers what confused them.
- Read sales call objections.
- Check where people drop off.
- Fix the biggest leak.

Then test larger changes with a clear hypothesis:

> Because we saw [evidence], we believe [change] will [outcome]. We will measure [metric] over [period].

Measure qualified leads, not just clicks. A page that produces more form fills but worse leads is not a win.

## A simple landing page wireframe

1. **Header:** logo, one-line navigation (or none), primary CTA.
2. **Hero:** headline, subhead, primary CTA, proof strip.
3. **Problem:** three specific pains.
4. **Solution:** how the offer solves them.
5. **What is included:** concrete deliverables.
6. **Proof:** case study or example with a result.
7. **Process:** three or four steps, including what happens after contact.
8. **Pricing signal:** starting range or "fixed quote after discovery."
9. **FAQ:** objections.
10. **Final CTA:** repeat the promise and the action.

This is not a template to copy blindly. It is an order of arguments.

## Common mistakes

- Clever headline, unclear offer.
- No proof.
- Too many CTAs.
- A form that asks for a budget before trust is built.
- A testimonial that sounds written by the vendor.
- Stock photography that could belong to any company.
- A mobile layout that hides the CTA.
- No success state after submitting.
- No tracking, so nobody knows what works.
- Sending paid traffic to a page with no message match.

## Key takeaways

- One page, one promise, one primary action.
- Put proof next to the claim.
- Answer objections in the order buyers raise them.
- Reduce form friction and make the page fast.
- Write in plain language and use images that show the work.
- Fix obvious friction before running complex tests.

## FAQ

### Do landing pages need SEO?

Paid campaign pages usually should be `noindex` if they duplicate an organic page. Organic service pages should be indexable and written for search intent. Do not send paid traffic to a page that competes with your main service page for the same query.

### Should I include pricing?

A starting range filters out mismatched buyers and builds trust. If you cannot publish a range, explain how pricing is scoped and what the next step is. "Contact us for pricing" alone creates friction.

### How many form fields are too many?

If a field does not help you reply or route the lead, remove it. For most service businesses, three to five fields is plenty.

### Do testimonials really help?

Real ones do. They reduce perceived risk by showing that someone like the buyer got a result. Fake or generic testimonials can backfire. If you do not have them, show the work instead.

### How often should I update a landing page?

Review it when traffic, offer, or competition changes, and after every campaign. Fix obvious issues immediately. Run larger tests on a schedule rather than redesigning constantly.
